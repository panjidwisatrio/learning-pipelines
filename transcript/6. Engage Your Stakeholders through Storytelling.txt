[Video description begins] Topic title: Engage Your Stakeholders through Storytelling. [Video description ends]

When technology professionals communicate with stakeholders, they are telling them stories. By developing your storytelling skills, you'll be better able to communicate effectively. Instead of thinking about what statistics or updates you need to pass on to your stakeholders, ask yourself, "What story am I trying to tell?" Instead of dryly outlining the implications of missing the deadline, tell the story of a customer who'll be disappointed because the new web site isn't launched on time. This turns your IT problem into a human dilemma that people can engage with. One reason why storytelling is so important is because our brains are better at remembering stories than statistics and facts. Stories make the statistics come alive. You can outline how your network upgrade will lead to fast broadband speeds. Or you can tell the story of a stressed out new father who, because of the faster broadband, no longer has to stay late in the office to complete his online administrative tasks.

A story is a narrative journey, and stakeholders become engaged in following your tale. The better you tell the story, the more likely they are to remember the facts that the story illustrates. When you tell a story, you are making abstract concepts more concrete. A compelling story can illustrate ideas in a way that a chart or graph simply can't. You can engage listeners by getting them emotionally invested in the development of a story. If you build suspense into your story, they'll want to know what happens next. Of course, you are not trying to turn your IT problem into a thriller, but you do want to humanize the issues involved. How will the issue affect employees or customers? How can you make their lives better? By making the issue more personal and relatable, you can better argue your case for change.

Of course, many people aren't born storytellers. Like most skills, storytelling requires training and practice. The more stories you tell, the more accomplished a storyteller you'll become. A successful, engaging story has a number of key ingredients. First, when you tell your story, you can draw on previous experiences. You could, for example, compare your current IT issue with a challenge you faced the previous year. This helps to put your story in context. Also, try to build in well-developed themes, plots, and characters. Obviously, you don't have to write a page turner, but aim to make your narrative compelling. Perhaps your theme is delivering excellent service to your clients. You introduce two different clients into your story to put faces to the issues you are encountering. Highlight the frustrations and challenges you faced on your project, and what you need to do to overcome them.

The one essential ingredient in all drama is conflict. Without them, there's no story. Include drama and conflict in the story of your project. They help you make the issue matter and hold stakeholder interest. What obstacles do you need to overcome? Who is standing in your way? And, most importantly, how can the stakeholders help you resolve the conflict? When communicating with stakeholders, use vivid, descriptive words that accurately reflect the situation. Avoid boilerplate text or generic statements. Vivid words catch people's attention and hook them into your story. For example, perhaps redundant data is "clogging up" your databases. Or maybe your app redesign will result in a "slick, pristine" interface. Or perhaps you have to deal with a "persistent chatter" of customer complaints about your online order forms.

Next, keep your stories tight and focused. If you want to convey how budget limits are frustrating your project, don't meander into discussions about supply chains or recruitment policies. Stick to your main narrative. Every detail that you include should be relevant to that narrative. Speaking of details, remember to keep them in check too. Remember the golden rule of storytelling: adapt your story for your audience. Don't overdo background or details. What details do the stakeholders need to know? Resist the temptation to show off technical expertise and jargon. Just include enough information to make your story believable and come alive, and put it in terms your audience will understand. When arguing the case for a new data storage solution, you don't need to give a history of cloud computing, just why your solution will benefit the company.

Be careful how you structure your story. People need to be able to follow your narrative. The classic structure of story is to have a beginning, middle, and end, and usually there's no good reason to stray from this formula. Begin by introducing the problem without lingering on the background. Then, in the middle, outline the various ways you tried to overcome the problem and the challenges you encountered. And end by showing how you resolved the problem, or what steps now need to take place to resolve it. Ideally, make your stakeholders key characters in this resolution. Finally, every great story has an engaging hero.

Try to introduce a key character around whom the story revolves. This again helps to humanize the message you want to convey. Weave a hero into the story. Your hero might be a team member who worked tirelessly to solve the problem. Or it could be a customer who brought an IT flaw to your attention, and engaged with your team to find a solution. Or it could even be a stakeholder who stepped in at the crucial moment to inject new life into a dying project. If you can turn your message into a compelling story, you're more likely to engage your stakeholders, secure their buy-in and support, and enlist their help in bringing the story a happy ending.
