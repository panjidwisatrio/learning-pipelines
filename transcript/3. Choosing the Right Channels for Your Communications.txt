[Video description begins] Topic title: Choosing the Right Channels for Your Communications. [Video description ends]

When communicating, you might assume the most important thing is to convey what you want to say. However, you also need to be mindful of what the recipient needs to hear. This applies to stakeholder communications as well. You must understand their information needs, and aim to meet them by carefully targeting your communications to them. So how can you effectively target your stakeholder communications? To do this, you must deliver the right message at the right time in the right format to the right people. As an IT professional, you have numerous communication channels at your disposal. When dealing with stakeholders, you're most likely to use meetings, presentations, conference calls, workstreams, instant messaging, or emails. It's important to understand which communication channels work best for you. However, you must also take into account your stakeholders' preferences. This helps ensure effective multidirectional communication between you and stakeholders. Although there are many different ways to communicate with stakeholders, they can be grouped into three general types: interactive, push, and pull communications.

First, consider interactive communication. As the name suggests, this involves two or more people interacting with each other. It's an exchange of information, rather than just one party telling the other party something. Because the parties are interacting and sharing information, this is a very effective form of communication. It enables everyone to clarify the information and reach a common understanding. Meetings are perhaps the most obvious example of interactive communication. Other forms include telephone and conference calls. This form of communication is very useful at the start of a technology project when you might want to brainstorm and gather ideas. For example, you might be looking for ways to make the corporate web site more engaging for prospects. Interactive communications with stakeholders, such as your digital marketing and product management teams, would be particularly helpful for gathering suggestions. The second form of communication is push communications.

Essentially, this is where one party pushes information to others. You would use this method when you want to tell someone something without engaging in a discussion about it. The advantage of this method is that it's an effective way to quickly update many people at once. The disadvantage is that you cannot be sure that your message has been properly understood, without then opening a discussion. Common push methods include text or instant messages, emails, or voicemails. For example, you might want to alert stakeholders to a technical glitch in an application and update them on a resolution of the problem. In this case, your aim is to inform and reassure people, rather than engaging with them.

The final form of communication is pull communications. In this case, it is up to recipients to pull the information from a central location. This is a very effective communications method when you need to update a large audience or when you have a lot of information to distribute. It is much less time-consuming than composing complex individual messages, every time you need to update stakeholders. Also, it comes across as less "pushy", because you are leaving it up to the recipients to decide when they will access the information. The obvious drawback is that you can't control when they read the information or be sure that they have understood it. Common pull communications methods include Intranet sites, project web sites, workstreams, and knowledge bases.

Pull communications are an effective way of keeping project team members up to date on project progress and changing priorities. If people from multiple teams are working together to revamp a web site, a central communication hub is a useful way to ensure everyone is getting the same information without flooding them with constant messages. This logically leads to the question of which one should you use. That depends on the circumstances. As you have seen, each method has its advantages and disadvantages. And, of course, you have to take into account which communication methods your stakeholders prefer. If you follow best practices for each communication method, you have a greater chance of ensuring that your message is received and understood. It's something of a cliché to say that everyone dreads meetings. However, badly run meetings can indeed lead to frustration for all involved. If you decide to use meetings to update your stakeholders, you need to ensure that each meeting has a clear purpose. Otherwise, it could just meander aimlessly and waste everybody's time.

A clear agenda is essential, so that everyone knows what is expected from the meeting. Agree an outcome with stakeholders, so that you assess whether the meeting was a success. Be respectful of everyone's time and keep your meetings short and focused. Stick to the agenda, and only discuss relevant points. If necessary, have a separate meeting later about peripheral points that arise. Also, keep a record of what was discussed in the meeting, noting agreed action points and any concerns that people raised. These action points can in turn become the basis of the next meeting's agenda. At times, you may want to use presentations to update your stakeholders. These can be more engaging than meetings, especially if you incorporate elements of multimedia.

Again, be respectful of people's time and keep presentations on point. Be clear about what you want your presentation to achieve and clarify how it will benefit the stakeholders. Grab their attention and get to the core material as soon as possible. Know your audience and work out how best to keep them engaged. Project enthusiasm and confidence show that you're passionate about the message, and make it clear why they should care too. Be mindful of your body language, looking engaged and enthusiastic, but don't let your movements or mannerisms become a distraction. Take time to practice your presentation, preferably in the space where you'll be delivering it. Make sure all the equipment you need is available and working and have a backup plan in case of unforeseen problems. In today's working environment, teams are often dispersed across many locations. As a result, conference and video calls have become a popular way of communicating with stakeholders.

When on a conference call without video, remember that the participants cannot see you, so tailor your communication for a listening audience. Speak slowly and clearly and use an unambiguous tone. Even if you're leading the call, aim to listen twice as much as you speak. Ensure everyone is given a reasonable opportunity to speak. And wrap up the call by reiterating what has been agreed, just as you would in a face-to-face meeting. If there are a lot of people on the call, make sure you use people's names, so that participants know who is being addressed or speaking, and make sure you know how to pronounce people's names correctly. Also, give people a chance to prepare for the call by sending any relevant materials in advance so that people are able to "read up" before joining the call.

New communications channels are emerging all the time. A recent development in workplace communication has been workstreams. These are collaborative platforms where team members and stakeholders can post updates, communicate, and share resources. Collaboration workstreams have the obvious advantage of providing a central space for all project communication. They ensure that all people have access to the same information. However, you do need to be careful when using them. They can become a huge time drain if you spend all your time reading all the updates. Rather than trying to keep up with the constant stream of information, consider just dipping in at key times during the day. Also, don't overly or exclusively rely on workstreams to communicate with stakeholders. Retain a "human touch" and contact them directly when appropriate. Also, avoid getting embroiled in long, winding discussions on the message threads. Keep messages brief and to the point.

Sometimes, it's better, and even more tactful, to take a discussion offline. Finally, don't forget the importance of email in stakeholder communications. Although it has almost become old fashioned nowadays, for many people, this is still the primary means of work place communication. When using email to communicate with stakeholders, bear in mind some best practices. Like all communication, your email messages should be concise and focused. Nobody has time to read a rambling stream of consciousness during a busy workday. Use clear subject lines, so people immediately know what the email is about. And put the most important information at the start of the email. This enables people to quickly decide whether they need to read the full email.

Also, remember to check your email before sending it. Have you included any necessary attachments? Are any links in your email working? And proofread the content. Mistakes in email can look unprofessional, and can lead to confusion, or even worse, misunderstanding. Carefully read the email and make sure you would be able to understand the email if you received it. Keeping stakeholders updated is an important part of the technology professional's job. Knowing how to effectively use the various communication channels available to you will help ensure that you and your stakeholders are able to communicate in a productive way.
