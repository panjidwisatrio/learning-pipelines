[Video description begins] Topic title: Establishing Trust through Difficult Communications. [Video description ends]

As a technology professional, you'll often have the need to communicate updates to your stakeholders. And sometimes the news you need to tell won't be positive. You must, however, resist the temptation to avoid or hide bad news. Instead, update your stakeholders honestly in a timely fashion. In this way, you can earn their trust and continued support. In any project, things can go wrong. And it'll be up to you to deliver the bad news. Maybe the budget is stretched, or there is the potential that a crucial deadline will be missed. Or you may be facing something more serious, such as the complete failure of an IT project. Maybe you need to alert your stakeholders to a worrying security breach.

Or you may just need to update them about some changes that they may feel uncomfortable about, such as leadership or team changes. It's perfectly natural to feel somewhat nervous about delivering bad news. However, being an excellent communicator involves delivering both good and bad news effectively. And you can even turn the experience to your advantage. For example, you could work with the stakeholders to forge a positive outcome from a crisis. Stakeholders will admire your honesty and quick thinking, which in turn can strengthen trust between you. Handling a crisis effectively gives you a chance to show how important technology is to the overall success of the organization.

There are some basic best practices to apply when you're delivering difficult communications to stakeholders. Always be direct. Don't try to "spin the story" or make it sound less serious than it is. Stakeholders need to know exactly what the bad news is. Be clear and concise. Update stakeholders on what has happened and what needs to be done. Get to the point as quickly as possible, being respectful of how they are likely to react. Be empathetic and try to understand their perspective. Finally, be sincere and genuine. Honesty is the best policy in these situations and, as noted before, how you handle the difficult communication can lead to greater trust between you and the stakeholders.

When delivering bad news, apply the TRIC formula. This means that your information is timely, relevant, important, and comprehensible. You can ask yourself a number of questions when trying to determine whether you have TRIC information. First, timely. Did the issue arise recently? Is it ongoing? And does the issue need to be addressed soon? Next, relevant. Is this information relevant to the stakeholders? Can they address this issue? And are you presenting it in the proper context, so that stakeholders can see why they should care about it? Third, important. How important is the issue? Is it the only important issue? And how important is it compared with other active issues?

Finally, comprehensible. Will the stakeholders understand the issue? Do they have the necessary background? Have you given them enough information to understand the issue? Are you presenting the facts clearly or do you risk confusing the stakeholders? And finally, do the stakeholders understand what you want them to do? Have you given them clear actions? Having decided that your information does indeed need to be passed on to the stakeholders, you need to decide how best to deliver it. There are several important steps to consider. The first step is to be prepared. You need to come across as being in control of the situation, so you don't want your communication to inadvertently unleash a panic. Don't let rumors take hold.

Ensure that you deliver quick and accurate information to the relevant stakeholders. Forward planning will be of great benefit in a crisis. Plan for different events and create distribution lists that you can refer to when the events occur. This means you don't waste time trying to decide who needs to know what. Have a crisis communication plan in place. Know which channels you'll use. Develop communication templates. These templates will enable you to quickly outline what happened, and its impact on the stakeholders. Make clear why the stakeholders should care about the issue and what action you and they need to take. And have a plan in place for follow-up communication as the crisis unfolds.

For example, suppose there has been a security breach in your organization's internal network. This is an event that most organizations would plan for, because although unfortunate, it is a common risk that many IT systems face. Your first step, then, would be to follow agreed protocols for communicating the crisis to your stakeholders. The second step is to gather the facts. Remember, rumors and lies are your greatest enemies during a crisis. Before you can communicate to others what has happened, you need to make sure that you yourself understand the situation. If you're unsure, contact people who do understand the situation, or were involved in it. Then decide what you need to communicate and whom you need to get in touch with. Although you don't want to cause unease by rushing out communications, do take into account the need to quell people's fears and allay their concerns, especially if rumors are already swirling around. This is particularly important during, for example, an IT security breach or a technology failure. What caused the security breach? What's the impact? And how can it be resolved?

Once you've gathered the facts and decided what you need to communicate, the third step is to determine the desired outcome of your communication. What do you want to achieve? Perhaps you just need to inform people about the situation and the actions you're taking. If a sense of crisis is taking hold in the organization, you might want to reassure people that everything is under control. In the case of a serious crisis or an emergency, your aim should be to direct people, making it clear to them what action you need them to take. In the example of a security breach, the obvious desired outcome is fixing the breach, preventing further breaches, and regaining the trust of your stakeholders. Obviously, you also need to focus on the content of your communication.

So your fourth step is to create a message. When communicating bad news, there's no room for ambiguity or confusion. Your message must be clear and easy to understand. Stakeholders will become even more uneasy if they can't understand what you're communicating. Also, although it's useful to have communication templates, don't rely on boilerplate, standard message. Customize your communication to the situation. In the network security breach example, even though you're likely to have ready-made templates in place for this eventuality, make sure your communications sound like a thoughtful response to this specific crisis. You don't want to sound like you're just repeating a previously prepared script. So, what should you include in your message? You need to include enough detail to clearly explain the situation, but not so much detail that you confuse or overwhelm the stakeholders. If you still lack some details, be honest about this and assure stakeholders that you'll update them as soon as you know more.

Make sure the stakeholders know why the communication is important. Highlight its impact on them. And be mindful of their concerns and doubts. But seek to reassure them and be positive. Make yourself available for follow-up questions using an appropriate channel, if necessary. And aim to end the communication on a positive, confident note. Remember, your aim is to show them that you have, or will have, the problem under control. Having created your message, your next step is to inform and educate. You have different stakeholders with different needs, and you need to update them all. Although all stakeholders should receive essentially the same message, you can tailor the amount of detail, depending on how much each stakeholder needs to know. Respect people's time and don't overload them with extraneous details. Also, bear in mind the corporate culture.

There may already be processes, or even etiquette, in place for communicating bad news. Make sure you adhere to these when informing and educating your stakeholders. In the case of a security breach, stakeholders are going to want to know why it happened, how it affects them, and what steps you're taking to remedy the situation. However, they don't need to know all the technical details of either the breach or the fix. Give stakeholders enough detail to assure them that you are in control of the situation. The sixth step is to sense and respond. This is basically following up on your message. You can follow up through formal and informal channels. First, try to get a sense of how your message was received. Was it understood properly? Did people react to it as you expected? Did anything unexpected crop up?

And second, respond as necessary to your stakeholders' reactions. Follow up with them to clarify the issues, if needed, or to address their queries or concerns. If they've made negative comments, work with them to come up with positive actions to turn the situation around. After a security breach, stakeholders are likely to have numerous questions and concerns. Take their concerns seriously and empathize with them. And answer their questions truthfully and as comprehensively as necessary. The seventh and final step is to adjust and update. A crisis is not a static event. Things unfold, escalate, and diminish almost by the hour. In the case of a network security breach, new threats might emerge as you deployed patch to deal with earlier threats. So remember to adjust your communications as the situation evolves.

Maintain an upbeat sense of control, and assure people that you're there to address their concerns. Provide the stakeholders with updates on the ongoing situation. Use your judgement to make sure you don't swamp them with an endless litany of minor updates. Your aim is to show how you’re reacting to a changing situation and keeping a handle on it. You could use stories to give concrete examples of how you or others are working to make sure the crisis is resolved as quickly as possible. Eventually, every crisis passes, and you finally have a moment to catch your breath.

However, don't wallow in complacent self-congratulation. This is your chance to calmly assess how you handled the situation and how effective your communication with stakeholders was. You can now return to the first step, be prepared. In light of the crisis that has just passed, how would you change how you prepare for the next crisis? Perhaps you need to update your distribution lists or communication templates. Also, take note of what was handled particularly well. You want these actions to become best practices in future crises. No one likes to be the bearer of bad news, but by following a process, you can be better able to communicate it to your stakeholders in ways that keep them fully informed, and fully supportive of the actions required, to get the project back on track.
