[Video description begins] Topic title: Honing Your Stakeholder Message. [Video description ends]

As a technology professional, you often have to advocate for your projects. This involves making your stakeholders aware of the value that they deliver. By effectively communicating your message to them, you can ensure greater engagement with and commitment to your project while also building rapport with them. There are many ways you can communicate your message, and your communication will often depend on your IT project's specific needs. However, the main goal is always to make stakeholders aware of the value of your project, specifically its value to them. When crafting your communication, bear in mind some key questions about your stakeholders. First, why should they care about your message? Make sure you understand why the initiative you're working on is important to them.

A stakeholder who cares about your project is a stakeholder who's engaged with it. And why should they care if they don't care? Sometimes, stakeholders might not be aware why your project is important to them. Clarify how it's goals align with their own goals. Next, what is the benefit of the information you're sharing with them? Remember, stakeholders don't need to know every detail about your project. Just share with them what's relevant to them, and make clear why the information is relevant to them. Problems and obstacles crop up in every IT project. And you often need stakeholder help to overcome these challenges. Therefore, what is the challenge you need them to engage in? Clarify exactly what problems you're encountering, and why these problems are important to your stakeholder. Stakeholders are unlikely to engage with problems that they feel don't concern them.

Finally, what is the expected outcome of your message? If you need the stakeholders to take action, make clear what action you expect them to take. And if the message is just for information and update purposes, make that clear. You will want to ensure that stakeholders both receive and understand your communication. To do this effectively, you must take all of these answers into consideration, and hone your message and its delivery. First, consider your actual message. How can you ensure that it is clear? First, and this is the golden rule of all effective communication, tailor your message to your audience. When you know to whom you're communicating, you know how much detail you need to go into. For example, if you're communicating at the executive level, the people are more likely to be interested in a strategic message. They want to know how your project enables the organization to meet its overall goals. Suppose you're updating your CEO about your data storage project.

The CEO is unlikely to care about the minutiae of virtual networks and cloud platforms. So, when communicating with the CEO, focus on how the project delivers value to the business by creating an efficient data storage and retrieval system. But if you're communicating at the operational levels, these people are interested in tactical and concrete details about the project. This would involve issues such as resources, delays, and team performance. Use fact-based messages when communicating with stakeholders. In other words, don't get lost in philosophical background or emotional concerns. Stick to the objective facts. This enables your audience to engage directly with the information, rather than having to sort the key information from the contextual musings. It's important that you keep your message consistent.

Stakeholders are likely to become concerned if you change your message without explanation. So, for example, if you have been telling your stakeholders that your project is important to the business, because it will ensure 20 percent faster connections to the core online databases, stakeholders will be puzzled, if you suddenly start saying that your project will deliver 10 percent faster connections. They will want to know why the connection speed has suddenly changed. But while being consistent is important, you must be mindful that things do change during a project. If circumstances dictate, your message should change also. Out of date information is of no use to anyone.

Remember that when communicating project changes, you must make it clear why the changes occurred and the impacts this will have on the project. So, returning to the previous example, if your project can deliver only 10 percent faster connection speeds, you need to explain the reasons for this. Perhaps that's the fastest speed you can deliver within the existing infrastructure. Effective communication with stakeholders is necessary to the success of any project. By honing your message to their specific needs, you can effectively explain and market your IT project, to ensure stakeholder understanding, buy-in and support.
